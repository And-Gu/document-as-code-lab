Discover how new ways of working can enable better collaboration, more automation, and new uses for AI agents—beyond individual documents and information scattered across or locked inside business systems.

### A situation you may recognize

A customer commitment is recorded in a PDF. The latest forecast is in a spreadsheet. Resource availability and budget information live elsewhere. Before a review meeting, someone has to bring those pieces together—and work out which differences matter.

This tutorial explores a different way of doing that work. The examples are deliberately small. They make the sources, connections, checks, and results visible so you can see what could change in your own environment.

## Chapter 1 bridge after the small opening example

The onboarding example is simple enough to inspect without learning a new business process. The same questions become more demanding when information comes from several places: Which source supports this statement? Do two labels describe the same thing? Is a date a commitment, a plan, or a forecast?

Document-as-code gives us one practical way to make those questions visible. It does not require every business system to be replaced, and moving a document into Git does not connect it to the rest of the organization. The value lies in how information is maintained, connected, checked, and reused.

## Chapter 5: meaningful structure

A planning system may already contain highly structured data. The difficulty begins when another team needs to combine its forecast with a customer commitment and a resource plan. A field named “date” is not enough: we also need to know what the date means, what it refers to, and where it came from.

## Chapter 7 bridge

The processing lab starts with sources that are already prepared. In everyday work, preparation is often part of the challenge. Names differ, dates have different meanings, and a useful piece of evidence may be missing. The information-refinement example lets you inspect that work before generating a briefing.

## Chapter 8: assistance beyond text

An assistant can help rewrite a status report. A tool-enabled agent could also help gather permitted inputs, compare a forecast with a commitment, identify gaps, and prepare a source-linked draft. That requires more than a writing prompt: the agent needs access to the right sources, their meaning, suitable tools, and clear limits on what it may change.

The walkthrough below illustrates that workflow with deterministic rules. It does not run an AI model or connect to a business system. You can inspect what the tools calculate and where a person would still need to decide.

## Refinement lab controls and feedback

Entry: **What should we check before communicating a changed delivery date?**

Disclosure: **Fictional sources. Changes stay in this browser session. No external system or AI service is connected.**

Primary actions: **Explore the sources**, **Use this mapping in the exercise**, **Build briefing**, **Reset example**.

Helpful secondary actions: **Show the worked example**, **Leave unresolved**, **Inspect source**, **Why this appears**, **How could an agent help here?**

Stale state: “The inputs have changed. This briefing still uses the previous inputs. Build it again to see the new result.”

Missing evidence: “The budget source is not included. A project cost comparison cannot be made from the remaining sources.”

Conflicting claims: “Two sources give different forecasts for the same delivery and period. Check their scope and authority before choosing a value.”

Mapping unresolved: “The planning extract uses IP-A. Its connection to DEL-101 has not been accepted for this exercise, so delivery-specific comparisons are withheld.”

Proposal: “The meeting note proposes 19 November. The supplied sources do not establish approval of that change.”

Agent label: **Illustrative agent workflow — deterministic replay, not a live AI run.**

## Chapter 11 closing

You do not need to copy this website's tools to recognize a useful opportunity. Start by identifying information that people repeatedly combine, check, or explain. Ask which sources already exist, which meanings and relationships need to be explicit, and what result would make the work easier to understand or perform.

The first experiment can use selected, reviewed extracts. A live integration would add questions about access, ownership, update frequency, and operational responsibility. The small lab helps you see those questions; it does not answer them automatically for your organization.
